The whole system in one read, from where an obligation is born to the moment you can prove you met it. Six acts.

Act 0 — Inception: where obligations come from

Nothing in this system originates inside the company. Every requirement traces back to something a legislature or regulator published, and that lineage has to be recorded or the register is just opinion.

Two questions become answerable only when that chain exists. Why does this control exist? Trace up. If you cannot, the control is orphaned — you are doing work with no recorded authority for it. Is it still required? If an instrument above it was superseded and nobody reviewed the descendants, you are enforcing a dead rule and calling it compliance.

Act 1 — Detection (stages 1 to 3)

A regulator publishes. We find out three ways: a scheduled monitor on publication pages, a licensee telling us informally, or industry press as a backstop. Automated monitoring is a net, not a guarantee — publication practice varies by country and page monitoring is brittle, so every market has a named human owner who triages every hit. Monitoring that is nobody's explicit job does not happen.

A human then classifies it: clarification, tightening, new obligation, or structural. The system can suggest from keywords. It must not decide.

Act 2 — Assessment (stages 4 to 6)

This is where the register earns its existence. Query it by market and affected category, and you get back every requirement touched, every control serving them, and every owner. Without that map you discover which processes were affected by finding out which ones broke.

Then the step almost nobody builds: the propagation check. Each control is tagged market-scoped or global. A global control shared by several markets means changing it for one changes it for all of them. Kenya's advertising and AML surfaces are both global — so tightening either for Kenya, without market-scoping first, either over-applies Kenyan rules to sixteen other countries or quietly misses the requirement. That single computation is the difference between a register and a risk system.

A human then sequences the work by regulatory exposure first, financial exposure second, friction last. Not by whichever is most annoying.

Act 3 — Implementation (stages 7 to 12)

Six stages, and four of them are the ones organisations skip.

Register and policy are updated as versioned artifacts with an approver, a change reason, and an effective date that is separate from the approval date. Config and workflow change next — market-scoped by default, so a regulatory change becomes a config change with an audit trail rather than a code release.

Then the SOP, and there is an automation whose only job is to catch its absence: if a workflow version changed and no SOP version changed with it, an exception is raised. That gap between what people are told to do and what they must do is the most common documentation failure there is.

Then training. A control that depends on people behaving differently does not exist until those people know. Completion is evidence, not admin — an auditor asks who was trained and when, and "we sent an email" is not an answer. Then QA and UAT, testing both that the control is correctly designed and that it actually operates, because those are different tests and they can disagree.

Finally the effective date, with its own countdown and its own blocking alert. Regulations have commencement dates. Being ready two weeks late is a breach even if the work was excellent.

Act 4 — Operation (stages 13 to 14)

Now the control runs, and exceptions start appearing. An exception is not a failure — an exception being raised is the control working. The failure mode is an exception quietly absorbed to hit a turnaround target.

Each one is classified and routed up a five-tier ladder. Missing information goes to operations, which may send two automated requests and no more — repeatedly asking for documents until something passes is a failure pattern dressed as diligence. Inconsistencies go to a compliance owner. Substantive concerns go to the designated officer and receive routing and nothing else: no automated request, no auto-close. If a case has a genuine concern, an automated document request tells the counterparty exactly what would make the problem disappear.

The decision is human, always, and it writes back to the register with its reasoning. If a human decision does not get recorded, the audit trail has a hole precisely where the important thing happened.

Act 5 — Proof (stage 15)

Six months later somebody asks: did this control operate? That is answerable only if three things existed at the moment it ran — the log entry, the actor, and the dated artifact tagged to the requirement. Missing any one and the control is unprovable, which in an audit is indistinguishable from absent.

This is why evidence is captured as activity happens rather than assembled when a report is due. Done that way, an audit is a query. Done the other way, an audit is a reconstruction from bank statements, inboxes and memory — and it becomes a crisis every cycle.

Alongside it runs the heartbeat: a daily check that every control produced evidence within its expected interval. A control that has silently stopped looks identical to one that is working, because nothing errors. Absence of errors is not evidence of operation.

And then it repeats

Stage 15 loops back to stage 1. The cycle never closes — regulators keep publishing, and each pass has to leave evidence behind. The system is not a project with an end date. It is a standing capability, and the measure of it is whether the next change takes days to assess instead of weeks.

What breaks it

Four failure modes, all of which this design is built to surface rather than hide. An unsourced requirement — nobody can say why it exists. A silent control — stopped operating, nothing errored. An unprovable control — it ran, but you cannot demonstrate that it did. A falling escalation rate — which looks like improvement and usually means operations has started absorbing judgement calls it should be passing up.

Each of those has a counter on the front page. They are meant to be uncomfortable.

Where obligations come from

Statute → regulator instrument → licence condition → requirement → internal policy → SOP → control → evidence. Everything above the licence condition is external and we do not control it. Everything below it we own entirely. A control that cannot be traced up that chain is orphaned — work with no known authority. A chain where an ancestor was superseded and the descendants were never reviewed means we are enforcing a dead rule.

The framing

A platform licensor typically holds no gaming licence and no customer funds — its local partners hold those. So the obligation is not "keep our licence." It is to ensure the platform lets each licensee satisfy what their regulator requires, across every market at once, without a change made for one market silently breaking another. That is a requirements-mapping and change-propagation problem, which is an operations discipline.

Three obligation buckets

Ours — we must do it. Theirs — the licensee must; we track that they can. Ours to enable — the obligation lands on the licensee but is unmeetable without a platform capability. The third bucket is where failures originate, because nobody owns it by default.

Two principles

Automate execution, not accountability. Execution is what a scenario does. Accountability is who answers for it. A workflow tool can hold the first and never the second. The corollary: automation should make escalation easier, not rarer. A falling escalation rate is a warning.

Audit preparation becomes continuous audit readiness. Evidence captured as activity happens makes an audit a query. Evidence reconstructed afterwards makes it a crisis.

Design and operating effectiveness are different tests

A control can be perfectly designed and never run. The dangerous quadrant is a control running reliably that checks the wrong thing — false assurance, which testing operation alone will never find.

Exception, incident, breach

An exception means a control fired — evidence it works. An incident means a control failed or was bypassed and we caught it. A breach means a requirement was not met. Rising exceptions with flat incidents is healthy. Few exceptions with occasional incidents means things are getting through.

What I bring, and what I would learn

Compliance operations across three regulatory environments: KYC and KYB across three regions at a blockchain fintech, compliance accuracy improved around 45%, no known breach through 3x client growth; grant and donor compliance at a nonprofit, where capturing evidence as activity happened cut audit-preparation effort roughly in half; and internal controls in current operations roles, including a data-integrity audit that recovered over $700,000 in unrecorded closed revenue.

I have not worked in iGaming. Certification and licensing processes, and formal regulatory horizon-scanning, are where I have least direct experience. The operating discipline transfers: understand the requirement, translate it into a process, assign ownership, capture evidence, catch exceptions, escalate what needs judgement, and measure whether any of it works.
