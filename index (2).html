Tier 3 receives routing and nothing else — no automated request, no auto-close. If a case has a substantive concern, an automated document request tells the counterparty exactly what would make the problem disappear. The corollary: automation should make escalation easier, not rarer. A falling escalation rate is a warning, not an improvement.

Every row crosses the line. Nothing crosses back. A scenario may package a case, route it, chase it twice and log every step — and then it stops, because the next act is a judgement and a workflow tool cannot be accountable for one. Automate execution, not accountability.

What must never be a scenario

  • Deciding whether a discrepancy is acceptable
  • Disposing a screening alert as true or false positive
  • Accepting a risk
  • Judging whether an expenditure is allowable
  • Determining an intervention for a player showing markers of harm
  • Closing a Tier 3 exception
  • Approving anything that requires four eyes

Naming the exclusions is more convincing than listing the inclusions. Anything on this list that appears in a scenario is a design defect, not a feature.

The four stages people skip

Stage 8 — SOP update. A process change without a documentation change creates a gap between what people are told to do and what they must do. SCN-12 raises an exception on any workflow version bump with no corresponding SOP version bump. That single automation prevents the most common documentation failure there is.

Stage 9 — training. A control that depends on people behaving differently does not exist until those people know. Training completion is evidence, not admin. An auditor will ask who was trained and when, and "we sent an email" is not an answer.

Stage 11 — effective-date tracking. Regulations have commencement dates. Being ready two weeks late is a breach even if the work was excellent. It gets its own countdown and its own blocking alert, separate from ordinary project tracking.

Stage 13 — post-launch monitoring. A new control is the most likely control to fail silently, because nobody has seen it run enough times to know what normal looks like. The heartbeat runs at elevated frequency for the first 90 days.

The register shows controls as rows. A row is not a control. These are the ten questions that make one real — answered in full for CTL-KYC-01, then applied as a template to every control in the register, where the unanswered questions are the finding.

Question 9 is the spine

Most controls fail it, and failing it means the control is unprovable — which in an audit is indistinguishable from absent. Answering it requires three things to exist at the moment the control runs:

  • What happened — the log entry
  • Who or what did it — the actor
  • What it produced — the evidence artifact, dated and tagged to the requirement

If any of the three is missing, the control cannot be evidenced retrospectively. 5 of 16 controls in this register are unprovable today. That number is a genuinely uncomfortable metric and it should be.

The framing

A platform licensor typically holds no gaming licence and no customer funds — its local partners hold those. So its obligation is not "keep our licence." It is: ensure the platform and the processes around it let each licensee satisfy what their regulator requires, across every market at once, without a change made for one market silently breaking another.

That is not a monitoring problem. It is a requirements-mapping and change-propagation problem, which is an operations discipline.

Three obligation buckets

Ours — the licensor must do it. Theirs — the licensee must do it; we track that they can. Ours to enable — the obligation lands on the licensee but is unmeetable without a platform capability.

The third bucket is where failures originate, because nobody owns it by default. A licensee failing an audit because the platform could not produce a required report is a commercial and reputational problem for the licensor even though the legal duty was never theirs.

Two principles

Automate execution, not accountability. Execution is what a scenario does; accountability is who answers for it. A workflow tool can hold the first and can never hold the second. Status, routing, reminders, evidence capture, escalation triggers, report assembly — automate all of it. Whether a discrepancy is acceptable, whether an alert is a true match, whether to accept a risk — those stay with a person who holds the authority. The automation layer tab states the boundary scenario by scenario.

Audit preparation becomes continuous audit readiness. If evidence is captured as activity happens, an audit is a query. If it is reconstructed afterwards from statements, inboxes and memory, an audit is a crisis. The difference is entirely process design.

Design and operating effectiveness are different tests

A control can be perfectly designed and never actually run. Testing must cover both. The dangerous quadrant is a control running reliably that checks the wrong thing — that is false assurance, and testing operation alone will never find it.

Exception, incident, breach

An exception means a control fired — that is evidence it works. An incident means a control failed to operate or was bypassed and we caught it. A breach means a requirement was not met. Rising exceptions with flat incidents is a healthy system. Few exceptions with occasional incidents means things are getting through.

What I bring, and what I would learn

I have run compliance operations in three regulatory environments: KYC and KYB across three regions at a blockchain fintech, with compliance accuracy improved around 45% and no known breach through 3x client growth; grant and donor compliance at a nonprofit, where capturing evidence as activity happened cut audit-preparation effort roughly in half; and internal controls in current operations roles, including a revenue and data-integrity audit that recovered over $700,000 in unrecorded closed revenue.

I have not worked in iGaming. There is sector-specific regulation I would need to learn — certification and licensing processes, and formal regulatory horizon-scanning, are the areas where I have least direct experience. The operating discipline transfers: understand the requirement, translate it into a process, assign ownership, capture evidence, catch exceptions, escalate what needs judgement, and measure whether any of it works.

Tooling

Deliberately low-code — Make.com and n8n for orchestration, Airtable for registers, Google Workspace for evidence. Compliance operations should not sit in an engineering queue: when a regulator changes a reporting format, the person who understands the requirement should be able to change the workflow that week. The trade-off is that low-code needs its own governance, because an undocumented automation nobody remembers building is a control nobody can test.
